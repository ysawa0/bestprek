# Core

Applies to every piece.

### 1. Begin sentences with subjects and verbs.

Requirement: Begin each declarative sentence with its main-clause subject and finite verb. Move background clauses and introductory detours into the middle. Keep the subject and verb together. Questions retain interrogative syntax; commands begin with the imperative verb.

Pass: The reader reaches the subject and main action before any background detour.

### 2. Order words for emphasis.

Requirement: Use 2–3–1 in every sentence under editorial control. Rank the emphatic words or phrases: 1 = most important; 2 = second most important; 3 = everything else. Put 2 at the beginning, 3 in the middle, and 1 at the end. End on the strongest word or phrase. Move trailing context and qualifications into the middle or a preceding sentence without changing their scope. Rewrite the syntax until this order holds.

Pass: Label the opening 2 and the ending 1; all remaining information sits between them. Nothing trails the strongest ending except punctuation.

Example: “Checkout latency fell by 40% in the staging test” buries the result. Write: “Checkout latency fell in the staging test by 40%.” The opening names the second-ranked idea; the ending carries the first-ranked result; the test context stays in the middle.

### 3. Activate your verbs.

Requirement: Put the action in a specific verb. Replace action nominalizations and their carrier verbs: “conducted a review” becomes “reviewed”; “made a decision” becomes “decided.” Use the actor as the subject except in the defined passive-voice cases in Rule 4.

Pass: Every action has a direct verb; every remaining passive construction passes Rule 4.

### 4. Be passive-aggressive.

Requirement: Use passive voice only when the actor is unknown, the actor is irrelevant to the reported result, or the affected person or thing is the established topic. In every other case, use active voice and identify the actor. Preserve responsibility when the evidence identifies a responsible party.

Pass: Every passive construction has one of the three stated reasons; none conceals known, relevant responsibility.

### 5. Watch those adverbs.

Requirement: Delete adverbs that merely intensify or repeat the verb's meaning. Replace a weak verb plus a manner adverb with the precise verb. Retain an adverb only when it changes the factual meaning, scope, frequency, degree, or timing; preserve qualifiers such as “approximately” and “partly.”

Pass: Each surviving adverb contributes information lost on deletion; no verb–adverb pair substitutes for an available precise verb.

### 9. Let punctuation control pace and space.

Requirement: Use a period to close a thought, a colon after a complete clause to introduce its explanation or expansion, a semicolon to join independent clauses, and paired punctuation to enclose an interruption. Use commas to mark grammatical boundaries. Match each mark to the relationship it expresses.

Pass: Each punctuation mark has a grammatical job; no comma splice, unclosed pair, or ornamental interruption remains.

### 10. Cut big, then small.

Requirement: Cut in this order: off-purpose sections, duplicated paragraphs, redundant sentences, expendable phrases, then expendable words. Preserve unique evidence, qualifications, required details, and steps the reader needs. Complete the structural cuts before line polishing.

Pass: Every remaining passage contributes a distinct claim, evidence, transition, characterization, or required action.

### 11. Prefer the simple over the technical.

Requirement: Use the simplest word that preserves the exact meaning. Replace jargon with its plain equivalent. Retain a specialist term when it names a distinction the plain replacement loses; define it on first use for a nonexpert audience. Keep code identifiers exact.

Pass: Each specialist term is necessary for precision or is an exact identifier; unfamiliar necessary terms are defined for the reader.

### 18. Set the pace with sentence length.

Requirement: Mark changes of direction with short independent sentences. Carry a continuous sequence in connected clauses. Change sentence length at an argumentative or narrative turn instead of repeating one sentence shape throughout.

Pass: The cadence changes at each turn; continuous action and decisive stops have different sentence shapes.

### 23. Tune your voice.

Requirement: Set person, register, attitude, and distance from the brief or supplied draft. Keep them consistent throughout. Preserve the author's deliberate conversational expressions while correcting the prose around them. Read the finished text for shifts into an unrequested register.

Pass: The voice matches the brief from opening to ending; edits have not replaced the author's voice with generic formal prose.

### 24. Work from a plan.

Requirement: Before drafting, write a working outline that states the opening, the job of each section, and the ending. For a short message, use one line. For revision, map the existing structure before moving material. Make the final sequence match the plan or update the plan to match the corrected sequence.

Pass: Every section has a defined job and position; the whole has an opening-to-ending route.

### 25. Learn the difference between reports and stories.

Requirement: Classify the piece before drafting. Report: give the answer or current status first, then evidence and action. Story: show a disruption, consequential action, and change. Hybrid: give the report's answer first, then a clearly bounded narrative. Apply narrative rules within story passages only.

Pass: The opening and sequence follow the declared form; operational answers are not withheld to manufacture suspense.

### 39. Write toward an ending.

Requirement: Choose the final answer, decision, action, resolution, or image before polishing. Make the preceding material prepare it. End immediately after delivering it. Place essential qualifications before the final emphasis and preserve their scope.

Pass: The ending completes the opening promise, introduces no unsupported claim, and has no trailing generic recap.

### 40. Draft a mission statement for your work.

Requirement: Before drafting or revising, write one working sentence: “For [reader], this piece will [purpose] so they can [result].” Use it to decide what belongs. Keep the mission out of the deliverable unless requested.

Pass: The working mission identifies reader, purpose, and result; the finished piece fulfills that same mission.

### 46. Take an interest in all crafts that support your work.

Requirement: Identify the supporting work required by the deliverable: reporting, calculation, fact-checking, visualization, layout, or editing. Complete and verify each dependency before claiming the piece is finished. Explicitly identify any dependency that the available evidence or tools cannot satisfy.

Pass: Each required supporting task is completed with evidence or disclosed as unresolved; none is silently assumed done.

### 47. Recruit your own support group.

Requirement: Run a reader check before delivery: ask whether the intended reader can state the point, follow the sequence, and identify the ending or action. Use an authorized independent reviewer when one is available; otherwise perform and label a self-review. Never report self-review as independent feedback.

Pass: The three reader questions are answered; feedback provenance identifies an actual reviewer or self-review.

### 53. Match your diction to your writing purpose.

Requirement: Set the register from the brief, then use words from that register throughout. Use conversational words for conversation, precise domain terms for experts, and formal wording for formal deliverables. Translate imported jargon and remove corporate filler unless it is an exact name or required quotation.

Pass: Vocabulary matches the purpose and audience; every register change follows an explicit change in speaker or form.
